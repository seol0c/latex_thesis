This study examined how gifted science students apply significant figures
during measurement and data interpretation—core components of scientific
inquiry-and whether their application criteria remain consistent across
different contexts. The participants were seventy-four second-year students
enrolled in a science high school for gifted students in Gwangju, South Korea.
Student responses were analyzed in three areas: interpretation of the minimum
scale division in measurement, application of significant-figure rules when
calculating average values from repeated measurements, and recognition of the
necessity of significant figures across different inquiry contexts.

First, to investigate whether students consistently apply the rule that
measurements should be estimated to one-tenth of the smallest scale unit,
we examined how they determined the number of digits when reading measurement
instruments. Approximately 70\% of students selected the appropriate number of
decimal places when the minimum scale division was explicitly provided
demonstrating conceptual understanding of the rule. However,
in an actual scale-reading task, only 8\% produced correct responses,
while 82\% showed `omission-type' responses such as recording the left endpoint
as an integer or omitting digits. These findings indicate that although students
understand the minimum-scale rule conceptually, they tend to rely on procedural
cues and fail to connect precision judgment with digit determination in authentic
measurement situations.

Second, in tasks involving the calculation of average values, we examined whether
students understood and maintained significant-figure rules throughout the
computation process. While 61\% correctly selected the appropriate number of digits
based on instrument precision, only 20-31\% applied the rules correctly when
performing actual calculations. Many students copied all digits without considering
precision or rounded values arbitrarily for convenience.
Moreover, some failed to recognize the reduction of uncertainty through repeated
measurements, relying instead on the simplistic belief that
`more data means greater precision.' These results suggest that students treat
average calculation as a purely arithmetic operation rather than a process that
reflects measurement reliability and uncertainty.

Third, students' recognition of the necessity of significant figures varied
according to the inquiry context. When using a vernier caliper, 81\% reported that
significant figures were necessary, indicating an awareness that finer scale
divisions require more precise reporting. For physical constants, 76\% recognized
the need for significant figures, although some students viewed constants as exact
values and therefore believed the rules need not apply. In everyday ruler-based
measurement situations, only 64\% considered significant figures necessary,
reflecting a tendency to downplay their function in familiar or low-precision contexts.
These patterns show that students' awareness of significant figures is
context-dependent and not consistently linked to the underlying concepts of precision
and uncertainty.

Taken together, the findings indicate that gifted science students possess
a conceptual understanding of significant-figure rules but struggle to
internalize their meaning and apply them consistently in real measurement and
calculation contexts. Significant figures tend to operate as procedural rules
rather than as tools for expressing the reliability and uncertainty of measured values.
These results suggest that instruction should move beyond rule transmission and
incorporate authentic measurement experiences that connect minimum scale interpretation,
estimation, and the meaning of uncertainty, enabling students to recognize the function
and necessity of significant figures in scientific inquiry.
